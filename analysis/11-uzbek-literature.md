# 11. Uzbek literature

A small but important shelf — your native-language tradition. Two literary works (Qodiriy and Hoshimov), one religious biography (*Islomga Bag'ishlangan Umr*), and the Quranic/scholarly works also referenced in [03 — Philosophy, religion, meaning](03-philosophy-religion-meaning.md): *Ulamolar nazdida vaqtning qadri*, *Ийман Ислам Куран*, *Clear Quran*, *Не Грусти!*.

This file covers the literary entries plus the religious biography.

---

## O'tkan kunlar — Abdulla Qodiriy

*("Days Gone By" / "Past Days" — 1922-1925, the first Uzbek novel)*

**Thesis.** A historical romance set in the late-Khanate period (mid-19th century Turkestan, before Russian conquest): Otabek and Kumush love each other and try to make a life together against the social, political, and family forces that finally crush them. Qodiriy invents the modern Uzbek novel and uses it to mourn — clearly but not sentimentally — a lost world that contained both injustice and a coherence the Soviet present has erased.

**Key ideas.**
- The first true *novel* in Uzbek prose; everything in modern Uzbek fiction descends from it.
- Late-Khanate Turkestan rendered in detail — courts, tea-houses, bazaars, the texture of *halol* and *haram*, the role of mothers-in-law and the *otin* (women's religious teachers).
- *Polygamy*, dramatized rather than argued: Kumush's poisoning by the second wife is the structural climax.
- Love as a real moral force in a society that does not officially recognize it.
- Qodiriy was executed by Stalin in 1938; reading him is itself an act against forgetting.

**Dangerous to forget.** Forgetting *O'tkan kunlar* means forgetting the *novel* — as a possibility — in your own language; and forgetting the Khanate as a place that had its own coherence rather than a "before" of Russian/Soviet rule.

**Test question.** Why does Qodiriy choose the *late Khanate* rather than the contemporary Soviet period as his setting? What argument does the choice make?

**Connections.** Spiritual cousin of *Anna Karenina* (love and society, with the social side rendered honestly). The Central Asian sibling of *И дольше века длится день* (Aitmatov — different language, same memory project). Distant: *Bugrov*'s pre-Soviet novels (off-list); world cousins are García Márquez, Achebe — first novels of late-colonial peoples.

## Dunyoning ishlari — Oʻtkir Hoshimov

*("The Affairs of the World" — 1982; a sequence of autobiographical/short prose vignettes)*

**Thesis.** A sustained meditation on Uzbek family, motherhood, village memory, and ordinary moral life — built from short, often standalone chapters, anchored in Hoshimov's own memory of his mother and the world she made. The book argues that the small, daily, mostly invisible labor of women is the substrate of everything else, and that to forget it is to forget where you came from.

**Key ideas.**
- *The mother* as moral and metaphysical center — not as figure of speech.
- Vignette form — each chapter complete in itself; the book accumulates rather than develops.
- Soviet-era Uzbekistan rendered without ideological frame; the texture is the politics.
- Childhood and old age held in the same paragraph; time as continuous, not linear.
- The deep Uzbek prose register — colloquial, proverbial, religious, and modern at once.

**Dangerous to forget.** This is your tradition's most-loved modern prose. Forgetting it is forgetting how to talk about home in your own language.

**Test question.** Why does Hoshimov write in vignettes rather than a single sustained narrative? What does the form make possible that a continuous novel could not?

**Connections.** Cousin of *Childhood* (Gorky — same project, different language and class). Sibling of *И дольше века длится день* (Aitmatov). World cousins: Annie Ernaux, Naguib Mahfouz's *Cairo Trilogy* — books where the texture *is* the argument. Background: *Не Грусти!*, *Quran*, *Islomga Bag'ishlangan Umr* — the religious-ethical world Hoshimov writes inside, even when not foregrounded.

## Islomga Bag'ishlangan Umr

*("A Life Dedicated to Islam" — biography of Shaykh Muhammad Sodiq Muhammad Yusuf, the major modern Uzbek scholar; Uzbek-language)*

**Thesis.** A devotional biography of the most influential Uzbek religious scholar of the late 20th and early 21st centuries — author of *Tafsiri Hilol*, *Ийман Ислам Куран*, and many other works that re-rooted Uzbek religious life after the Soviet rupture. The book argues that a single scholar's life of disciplined learning, teaching, and patient public engagement can re-thread a community's tradition across a generational break.

**Key ideas.**
- Tradition can be transmitted across discontinuity — the Soviet decades did not finally sever the chain.
- Scholarly biography is itself a *genre of religious instruction* in Islam (the *tabaqat* tradition); the form carries the lesson.
- Patience and pedagogical care over polemic — the book's emphasis is on the scholar's habit of dialogue and Quranic exposition rather than political confrontation.
- A modern Uzbek vocabulary for religious learning, partly his invention.
- Personal habits (early rising, daily reading, public approachability) presented as imitable.

**Dangerous to forget.** You'll forget a specific, recent, locally-rooted exemplar of how to be a learned Muslim in the modern world — replaceable in your mind only by abstractions.

**Test question.** Name one habit or pedagogical practice from the biography you would actually adopt, and the religious reasoning behind it.

**Connections.** Direct companion to *Ийман Ислам Куран*, *Clear Quran*, *Не Грусти!*, *Ulamolar nazdida vaqtning qadri*. Genre cousin: *Tuesdays with Morrie* (life-as-curriculum), *Surely You're Joking* (the working-life-as-instruction form). Counter-temperament: *Mortality* (Hitchens) — same question, opposite tradition.

> Note: I'm working from the general shape of the genre and the scholar's reputation; verify the specific author and emphases against your copy.

## Across this file

The literary side and the religious side, one project. Both Qodiriy and Hoshimov are doing memory-work for an Uzbek world the dominant 20th-century narratives (imperial, Soviet, then post-Soviet) wanted to overwrite. Reading them is itself a small political act.

If you forget everything else, keep two contrasts:

- **Qodiriy vs. Hoshimov** — the *novel* form (historical, plotted, epic) vs. the *vignette* form (autobiographical, accumulative, intimate). Both are how Uzbek literature claims its own subject.
- **Hoshimov + Aitmatov** — across languages (Uzbek, Russian-Kyrgyz), the same Central-Asian commitment: don't be the *mankurt*. Remember what you are.

The connection upward to your religious shelf (*Iyman, Islom, Quran*; *Islomga Bag'ishlangan Umr*; *Не Грусти!*; *Ulamolar nazdida vaqtning qadri*) is real and reciprocal — Qodiriy's moral world and Hoshimov's emotional world are unintelligible without the religious tradition, and the religious texts gain a real-world shape only when read alongside the literature.
