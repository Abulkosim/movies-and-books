# Action, Heists & Stylized Violence

What's the pleasure of watching skill under pressure? These films treat action as choreography — competence porn, body-as-instrument, problem-solving at high speed. The good ones are about something underneath; the fun ones don't pretend to be.

---

## John Wick 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 (2014–2023)

**What it's really about** — Grief processed through ritualized violence inside an underworld whose codes are more morally legible than the real world's. The franchise's actual achievement is the worldbuilding — the High Table, the Continental, the gold coins — which gives violence a moral grammar most action films can't be bothered with.

**Key scenes**
- I: "It wasn't just a puppy."
- II: The Rome catacombs.
- III: The horseback / motorcycle highway sequence.
- IV: The Sacré-Cœur stairs.

**Easy to miss** — The franchise gets quieter as it gets bigger. The first film was a small revenge story; by IV, it's an operatic existential meditation on whether John can ever stop. The closing scene of IV — the question of whether he is at peace — is earned by all four films.

**The test** — Why is the franchise about a dog?

**Connections** — *Drive*, *The Gray Man*, *Kill Bill*-adjacent.

## The Gray Man (2022)

**What it's really about** — A perfectly competent contemporary action film with no thematic ambition beyond delivery. Worth watching for genre fluency rather than insight. The Russo brothers' grammar is Marvel-formed and shows it.

**Key scenes**
- The Prague tram sequence.
- The plane fall.
- The fountain set-piece.

**Easy to miss** — The film is functionally a streaming-era genre exercise. Watching it as a study of how Netflix produces "blockbuster" mass entertainment is more interesting than watching it as drama.

**The test** — What does the film leave out that an older action film would have included?

**Connections** — *Red Notice*, *John Wick*, *Bourne* trilogy.

---

## Red Notice (2021)

**What it's really about** — A self-aware genre product that knows what it is — three movie stars on a streaming budget with a plot constructed entirely from heist-film building blocks. The pleasure is the cast chemistry, not the story.

**Key scenes**
- The opening museum heist.
- The bull-pen jail break.
- The various double-crosses.

**Easy to miss** — Nothing. The film is exactly what it appears to be. That can be a virtue.

**The test** — Why was this on Netflix instead of in theaters?

**Connections** — *The Gray Man*, *Ocean's Eleven*, *National Treasure*.

## Baby Driver (2017)

**What it's really about** — A getaway driver who uses music to drown out tinnitus from a childhood crash. The film's structural conceit — every action beat synced to a song on Baby's iPod — is also its thematic claim: he is not present in the violence, he is in the soundtrack. The film is about a man trying to live inside his headphones.

**Key scenes**
- The opening "Bellbottoms" car ballet.
- The coffee-run long take to "Harlem Shuffle."
- Doc's reversal.
- The final stairwell shootout.

**Easy to miss** — Baby is a child stolen by the genre. The film keeps showing us flashes of his mother's death, the ear-ringing, the loops on his recorder. He is not cool; he is dissociated, and the style is the dissociation.

**The test** — Why does Baby surrender at the end?

**Connections** — *Drive*, *La La Land*, *Pulp Fiction*.

## Die Hard (1988)

**What it's really about** — The genre prototype most modern action films are trying to recover from. McClane is a working-class New York cop in a building of L.A. corporate types getting robbed by Eurotrash; the class comedy is at least half the film. Modern viewers tend to read it as "just a great action film" — it was, and it was also a satire.

**Key scenes**
- The vent crawl.
- "Now I have a machine gun. Ho-ho-ho."
- The Hans-on-the-roof reveal.
- The taped-gun finale.
- The "yippee-ki-yay" moment.

**Easy to miss** — Ellis (the cocaine-snorting hostage) is the film's portrait of yuppie self-importance — the kind of guy McClane is set against. His death is the film's class-ideology punchline.

**The test** — Why is Hans in this office, specifically?

**Connections** — *Speed*, *John Wick*, *The Wolf of Wall Street* (counter-text).

## Legend (2015)

**What it's really about** — The Kray twins as a study in violent codependence. Tom Hardy plays both — Reggie the controlled, Ronnie the unhinged — and the film argues that English postwar masculinity produced two halves of one disorder, attempting to share a city and a body.

**Key scenes**
- The first joint pub appearance.
- Frances's voiceover.
- The hammer attack.
- The split-screen brother fight.

**Easy to miss** — Frances is the moral narrator and a victim of both brothers. Her suicide is the film's heart and the moment Reggie's myth dies for the audience. The film is more about her than its title suggests.

**The test** — Whose legend is being told?

**Connections** — *Goodfellas*, *Peaky Blinders*, *The Wolf of Wall Street*.

## No Limit (2022)

**What it's really about** — A French freediving drama based on real lives. The film is about the way extreme sports can be a vehicle for a coercive relationship — the coach is also the partner, the records are also the tether, the depth is also the danger he's putting her in. The thriller layer is real, but the relationship is the actual subject.

**Key scenes**
- The first dive together.
- The training-day power exchanges.
- The fatal record attempt.

**Easy to miss** — The film is a thinly fictionalized real story. Watching with that knowledge changes its weight; the genre tropes are not invention.

**The test** — Whose record was being chased?

**Connections** — *Whiplash*, *Black Swan*, *Babygirl*.

## Captain America (films)

**What it's really about** — The franchise is about the America-as-aspiration myth, played straight. Steve Rogers is the version of the country its story-songs sing about, which is also why the films are at their best when the country fails him (*The Winter Soldier*) and at their worst when it doesn't (*The First Avenger*).

**Key scenes**
- *The First Avenger*: the bomb-grenade in basic training.
- *The Winter Soldier*: the elevator fight.
- *Civil War*: the airport battle.

**Easy to miss** — *The Winter Soldier* is structurally a 70s paranoia thriller (think *Three Days of the Condor*) wearing Marvel paint. It is the franchise's best film and its least Marvel-formed.

**The test** — Which version of America does Steve refuse to serve?

**Connections** — *Spider-Man*, *Batman Begins*, *The Dark Knight*.

## Venom (2018) and sequels

**What it's really about** — A buddy comedy in superhero clothing. Tom Hardy and the symbiote are functionally a divorced couple working through codependence. The films work to the extent you accept that and fail when they pretend to be serious.

**Key scenes**
- The lobster-tank scene.
- The first "bonding" voiceover.
- Most of the comic timing in II.

**Easy to miss** — The films are not about Spider-Man or any larger universe. The franchise's pleasure is the two-character chamber piece.

**The test** — Whose voice is "Venom"?

**Connections** — *Spider-Man*, *Captain America*.

## Spider-Man (the various Sam Raimi / Marc Webb / MCU continuities)

**What it's really about** — Coming-of-age myth in a costume. Across iterations, the steady spine is grief and responsibility — Uncle Ben's "with great power" rephrased every cycle. The films are interesting as tonal barometers: each generation gets the Spider-Man it asked for.

**Key scenes**
- Raimi: the upside-down kiss; "you cannot win."
- Webb: the Stacy clock-tower fall.
- MCU: the Aunt May scene in *No Way Home*; the bridge with all three Spider-Men.

**Easy to miss** — The films keep being about loss of a parental figure. The recurrence is structural — the genre needs the wound to keep running.

**The test** — Which Spider-Man is yours, and what does that say about you?

**Connections** — *Captain America*, *Batman Begins*, *The Dark Knight*.

## Kung Fu Panda 4 (2024)

**What it's really about** — The series's formal demand: how does a master become a teacher? The film is uneven but its core question — when do you stop being the chosen one and start choosing the next one? — is mature for a children's franchise.

**Key scenes**
- The first encounter with the new villain.
- Po's hesitation about succession.
- The closing torch-pass.

**Easy to miss** — The franchise's overall arc is one of the more thoughtful Buddhist parables in mainstream animation. Po's progression — student, master, teacher — is structured by Zen pedagogy without making a point of it.

**The test** — What does Po have to give up to gain a successor?

**Connections** — *Spring, Summer, Fall, Winter… and Spring*, *Spirited Away*.

## Home Alone 1 & 2 (1990 / 1992)

**What it's really about** — The fantasy of being unsupervised, written for children and watched by adults nostalgic for it. The home-defense traps are the surface; the actual spine is loneliness — Kevin's relief at being alone curdles into the longing to be remembered. The Plaza Hotel sequence in II is the same emotion writ large.

**Key scenes**
- The aftershave scream.
- The pizza-rental delivery.
- The pigeon lady in the park.
- The closing reunion.

**Easy to miss** — The films are explicitly Christmas films. The traps are pretext; the religious-comfort score and the parental-reunion arcs are the films' actual work.

**The test** — Why is this a Christmas film, specifically?

**Connections** — *It's a Wonderful Life*, *Love Actually*.

## Man vs. Bee (2022)

**What it's really about** — Rowan Atkinson rebuilding *Mr. Bean* for the streaming era. The plot is slapstick around a man, a bee, and a luxury house he is house-sitting. The pleasure is character physicality; the depth is none, and the show is honest about that.

**Key scenes**
- The opening assignment.
- The escalating destruction.
- The dog.

**Easy to miss** — The show is a mid-pandemic project — a single-location, small-cast piece designed for COVID-era production. Its constraints are part of its texture.

**The test** — Why does the genre still work?

**Connections** — *Mr. Bean*, *Home Alone*.

## Enola Holmes (2020 / 2022)

**What it's really about** — Sherlock's younger sister as feminist coming-of-age inside a Victorian mystery box. The films treat the period setting not as decoration but as the obstacle Enola actually solves — the suffrage politics in II are the actual case, with the kidnapping plot a vehicle.

**Key scenes**
- The bicycle escape from finishing school.
- The fourth-wall-breaking direct address.
- The match-factory subplot in II.

**Easy to miss** — The films are aimed at a young female audience and refuse to tone down the period's misogyny in order to make the protagonist look heroic. The villainy is institutional.

**The test** — Why does Enola refuse Sherlock's offer of partnership?

**Connections** — *Pride and Prejudice*, *Knives Out*, *Pretty Woman*-counter-text.

---

## The Take (2016)

**What it's really about** — A standard Idris-Elba-led Paris-set thriller about a CIA agent and an American pickpocket caught in a terrorism plot. Genre-fluent, politically thin. Watchable.

**Key scenes**
- The opening pickpocket sequence.
- The Bastille Day setup.
- The rooftop chase.

**Easy to miss** — The film has thin engagement with the politics of contemporary Paris terrorism — it borrows the iconography without committing to a position.

**The test** — What does the film need Paris to be?

**Connections** — *The Gray Man*, *Red Notice*, *Bourne*.

## Taxi (Luc Besson, 1998 and sequels)

**What it's really about** — A French mass-market action-comedy franchise about a cabbie who keeps helping the police because his soup-bowl Marseille life requires the side-cash. The films are pure genre comfort food and have aged with mixed grace; their racial humor is uneven.

**Key scenes**
- The first chase.
- The bank-robbery getaway.

**Easy to miss** — The franchise's appeal is regional pride — Marseille's specific car-and-soccer culture as character. American viewers tend to miss the local detail.

**The test** — Why is the cab a Peugeot, specifically?

**Connections** — *Baby Driver*, *Drive*, *The Transporter*.
