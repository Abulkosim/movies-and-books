# Russian, Soviet & Slavic Cinema

A national cinema with its own questions: what does Russia ask of its sons and daughters? What does the country owe its dead? Watching Russian films, you keep meeting the same protagonists across a century — the disillusioned soldier, the patient wife, the man who reads too much, the boy from the courtyard.

---

## Москва слезам не верит / Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears (1980)

**What it's really about** — Three young women arrive in Moscow in 1958 with different ambitions; the film checks in twenty years later and asks what each of them got. The film is honest about gender, class, and the limits of female "having it all" in late-Soviet life.

**Key scenes**
- The early factory-dorm scenes.
- Katya's pregnancy and abandonment.
- The twenty-year jump.
- The Gosha train-station romance.

**Easy to miss** — Gosha is, on rewatch, surprisingly chauvinistic. The film celebrates him because the cultural moment did. Modern viewers can hold both — the warmth of Katya's story and the cost of Gosha's terms.

**The test** — Why does the title address the city, not a person?

**Connections** — *The Cranes Are Flying*, *Anna Karenina*, *The Notebook*.

## Brat 1 (1997) & Brat 2 (2000)

**What it's really about** — Russia's collapse decade told through one quiet, dangerous young man. Danila Bagrov is the post-Soviet everyman — not ideological, polite to elders, capable of horrific violence — and the films are the country's clearest portrait of what the 90s did to its sons. *Brat 2* turns explicitly nationalist; the diptych is less moral arc than political drift.

**Key scenes**
- I: The St. Petersburg apartment-block tracking.
- I: "Tell me, American, where is the strength?"
- II: The Chicago confrontation.
- II: The ending monologue.

**Easy to miss** — *Brat 2*'s ideology is more visible from outside Russia. The film's "America bad / Russia true" current is sincere, not satirical. Watching with that recognition matters; the second film is a cultural document of a specific late-90s mood that hardened into the next two decades.

**The test** — What does Danila actually believe?

**Connections** — *Слово пацана*, *Taxi Driver*, *Drive*.

## Слово пацана / Слово пацана. Кровь на асфальте (2023)

**What it's really about** — Late-Soviet Kazan teenage gangs (the Kazan phenomenon) as the seedbed of post-Soviet criminality and contemporary Russian masculinity. The series is unflinching about the cost — boys destroyed by their own code — and was a cultural event in Russia precisely because it photographed something the country preferred to forget.

**Key scenes**
- The first "joining" rite.
- The classroom-vs.-courtyard double life.
- The girlfriend subplot's escalating violence.
- The 1989 finale.

**Easy to miss** — The show was nearly censored in Russia for showing too much. Its existence is a product of a brief window in 2023; it would not be greenlit now. Read it as historical document and as cultural risk-taking simultaneously.

**The test** — Why is the title about *the word* (слово) of the boy?

**Connections** — *Brat 1*, *Goodfellas*, *Peaky Blinders*, *13 Reasons Why*.

## Двадцать шесть дней из жизни Достоевского (1981)

*(Primary entry in 14-biographies.md.)*

In this group: the film is the bridge between Russian literary canon and Soviet cinematic taste. Watching it slots Dostoevsky into the broader project of Soviet biopics that wanted to claim 19th-century writers as proto-revolutionary heroes. The film is partly that project and partly a quiet love story.

**Connection to this file** — *Anna Karenina*, *Crime and Punishment*, *Brothers Karamazov*, *Master and Margarita*.

## Нулевой пациент / Zero Patient (2022)

**What it's really about** — The 1988 Elista pediatric HIV outbreak in Soviet Kalmykia and the state's attempt to bury it. The series treats the Soviet medical-bureaucratic instinct (deny, classify, intimidate) as the actual disease; the virus is the proximate cause of death, the system is the cause of the cover-up.

**Key scenes**
- The first hospital cluster.
- The doctor's epidemiological tracing.
- The KGB confrontations.
- The eventual public disclosure.

**Easy to miss** — The series is structurally about how late-Soviet society normalized the unspeakable. The HIV crisis is the explicit subject; the implicit subject is everything else the state buried.

**The test** — Why was the outbreak in Elista, and why did Moscow react the way it did?

**Connections** — *The Lives of Others*, *Chernobyl* (HBO), *Dahmer* (institutional failure).

## Trigger (Триггер, 2018– )

**What it's really about** — A Russian psychotherapist who uses provocative confrontational technique. The show is a procedural about contemporary Russian psychic injuries — the ones a polite culture refuses to name — used as case-of-the-week framework.

**Key scenes**
- The first "trigger session" reveals.
- The protagonist's own backstory unspooling.
- The ethical-board confrontations.

**Easy to miss** — The show is interesting as a window into what Russian therapy culture, only recently emergent, treats as the dominant disorders. The frequency of male-shame and family-secret cases is a national mirror.

**The test** — Whose pain is the show photographing?

**Connections** — *In Treatment*, *Adolescence*, *Baby Reindeer*.

## Внутри Убийцы / Inside the Killer (2024)

**What it's really about** — A Russian profiling procedural in the *Mindhunter* / *Criminal Minds* tradition, with regional specificity. The investigator's interest is not the crime but the perpetrator's interior, and the show argues that contemporary Russia has produced a particular psychology that requires its own vocabulary.

**Key scenes**
- The interview-room set pieces.
- The investigator's own family intrusions.

**Easy to miss** — The show's debt to Western procedurals is explicit. Its differentiation is in the texture of contemporary Russian middle-class life — the apartment buildings, the commute, the food.

**The test** — What does the Russian setting do to a genre invented by FBI-era American TV?

**Connections** — *Memories of Murder*, *Mindhunter*, *The Silence of the Lambs*.

## Anna Karenina (multiple adaptations of Tolstoy)

**What it's really about** — A 19th-century woman's love affair, told from inside the social mechanism that destroys her. Anna's tragedy is structural: the same "fall" is forgiven in men (Stiva's adultery opens the novel) and lethal in women. Tolstoy is honest about that asymmetry and inconsistent about how much he sympathizes with it.

**Key scenes** *(adaptation-dependent)*
- The Vronsky horse-race fall.
- The opera-box humiliation.
- The train-station finale.

**Easy to miss** — The novel's parallel plot — Levin and Kitty — is half the book. Most adaptations cut it heavily. Without Levin, the novel becomes a tragedy of one woman; with him, it becomes a comparative study of what marriage can be.

**The test** — Why does Tolstoy give us Levin?

**Connections** — *Brothers Karamazov*, *Crime and Punishment*, *Madame Bovary*-adjacent, *Pride and Prejudice*.

## Master and Margarita (Bulgakov; the recent 2024 film)

**What it's really about** — Stalin's Moscow visited by the devil. The novel — and the new film — is a satire of literary-political cowardice, a love story, a meta-fictional argument about Christ, and a Faustian comedy, all at once. The 2024 adaptation is unusually willing to read the political content of the original directly.

**Key scenes** *(in the 2024 film)*
- Woland's first appearance at Patriarch's Ponds.
- The Pilate-Yeshua interludes.
- The ball.
- The flying-Margarita sequence.

**Easy to miss** — The 2024 film was attacked by Russian state media on release for being insufficiently patriotic. That attack is a kind of credential: the film read the novel honestly enough to discomfit the authorities the novel was originally banned by.

**The test** — Whose master is the title?

**Connections** — *Brothers Karamazov*, *Crime and Punishment*, *Anna Karenina*, *Faust*.
