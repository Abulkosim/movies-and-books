# Tech, Surveillance & Dystopia

The system as character. These films and shows ask what the platforms, the cameras, the rating apps, the algorithms, the totalitarian states — what they all want from us and what we hand over without noticing.

---

## Black Mirror (series overview)

**What it's really about** — Each episode is a thought experiment: take one technology, push it to its logical end, and ask what the human cost looks like. The anthology format is the point — there is no recurring lesson, only a recurring method. The show is most useful as a provocation, not a prediction.

**Easy to miss** — The show's worst episodes share a flaw: they confuse "the technology is bad" with "humans are bad." The best episodes know the technology is just exposing what was already there.

**The test** — Which episode best argues that the tech is the symptom, not the disease?

## Common People (Black Mirror, S7)

**What it's really about** — Healthcare-as-subscription, taken seriously. A woman's brain implant requires an ever-pricier tier to keep working; her partner takes on debt and worse. The episode is about the way American medical pricing already works, with a sci-fi gloss thin enough to see through.

**Key scenes**
- The first "lite tier" upgrade.
- The mid-conversation glitches.
- The husband's stream-for-money sideline.
- The final tier.

**Easy to miss** — The episode is structurally a Faustian bargain that started years before the protagonists realized they had signed it. The "free trial" is the whole horror.

**The test** — When did the bargain actually start?

**Connections** — *Nosedive*, *Black Museum*, *Idiocracy*.

## Eulogy (Black Mirror, S7)

**What it's really about** — Memory as walkable space. A man enters his own photographs to grieve a woman he treated badly. The episode argues that grief without the courage of self-correction is just nostalgia — and that a memory you are willing to revise is the closest you get to forgiveness.

**Key scenes**
- The early "she's just a face" stage.
- The slow recovery of detail in successive entries.
- The final identification of the figure he kept blurring out: himself.

**Easy to miss** — The "guide" he speaks to throughout the episode is built from her — a small, bitter detail that lands twice.

**The test** — What does Phillip have to admit before the photograph completes?

**Connections** — *Eternal Sunshine*, *Aftersun*, *Black Museum*.

## Nosedive (Black Mirror, S3)

**What it's really about** — A culture in which every interpersonal interaction is rated, and people's lives are stratified by score. It's a satire of social media as social-credit system, and a study of how performative sweetness corrodes selfhood.

**Key scenes**
- The plane-rebook collapse.
- The trucker pep talk.
- The wedding meltdown.
- The jail-cell freedom of yelling everything you actually think.

**Easy to miss** — Lacie's "freedom" at the end is partial. She is in jail. The episode is honest that escape from the rating system requires dropping out of society entirely — there is no "moderate" exit.

**The test** — What does Lacie actually want, before and after?

**Connections** — *The Truman Show*, *The Social Dilemma*, *Common People*.

## Black Museum (Black Mirror, S4)

**What it's really about** — Three nested stories of people commodified by technology — a doctor who becomes addicted to others' pain, a woman digitized into her husband's brain, a man whose consciousness is sold as a death-row tourist attraction. The frame's twist (the curator gets what he gave) is the show's clearest moral grammar lesson: every technology that turns a person into content has an audience.

**Key scenes**
- The doctor's first "patient empathy" trial.
- The husband's slow rejection of his digitized wife.
- The execution-tourism stand.
- The final reveal of who the visitor is.

**Easy to miss** — The episode is also a meta-commentary on the show itself. Black Museum *is* Black Mirror — a curated cabinet of human suffering presented as entertainment.

**The test** — Whose museum is it?

**Connections** — *Nosedive*, *Common People*, *The Truman Show*, *Eulogy*.

## The Social Dilemma (2020)

**What it's really about** — Recovering Silicon Valley insiders explain the platforms they built. The film's argument is that attention is a renewable resource being mined unsustainably, and that the people who built the mines now want them shut down. Worth pairing with criticism of the documentary itself, which has been faulted for absolving the same insiders it interviews.

**Key scenes**
- The teen-watching-phone dramatized scenes.
- The "three goals of the algorithm" diagram.
- Tristan Harris's testimony segments.

**Easy to miss** — The dramatized family scenes are weak compared to the interviews; the documentary is interesting almost despite its filmmaking. The interviews are the asset.

**The test** — Whose accountability does the film evade?

**Connections** — *The Social Network*, *Nosedive*, *Don't Look Up*.

## The Social Network (2010)

**What it's really about** — Not Facebook. The film is about a class-bitter young man who discovers he can engineer connection at scale specifically because he himself cannot connect. The Aaron Sorkin / David Fincher diagnosis is that the platform is a revenge fantasy that worked.

**Key scenes**
- The opening dialogue with Erica Albright.
- The Winklevoss "Gentlemen of Harvard" scene.
- The depositions intercut with the founding.
- The closing shot — refresh, refresh, refresh.

**Easy to miss** — Eduardo isn't only a victim. The film is fairer to Mark than its reputation; it shows Eduardo as out of his depth and Mark as the only person with a coherent vision. The tragedy is that the vision is built on rejection.

**The test** — What is Mark trying to prove, and to whom?

**Connections** — *Steve Jobs*, *The Wolf of Wall Street*, *Whiplash*.

## Don't Look Up (2021)

**What it's really about** — The climate emergency rendered as comet-impact metaphor and dressed as a satire. The film argues that the problem is not knowledge — everyone knows — but the social and media architecture that punishes anyone who treats knowledge as urgent.

**Key scenes**
- The morning-show appearance.
- The president's "let's just sit tight" briefing.
- The "don't look up" rallies.
- The final dinner.

**Easy to miss** — The closing dinner-table scene is the real argument: the family chooses presence over "doing something" because doing something has been made structurally impossible. It's a film that ends in private grace because public action failed.

**The test** — Why does the film end at the dinner table?

**Connections** — *Idiocracy*, *The Social Dilemma*, *Three-Body Problem*.

## Idiocracy (2006)

**What it's really about** — A satire that has aged into prophecy. Mike Judge's central claim — that the structures of mass culture would eventually optimize for the lowest common cognitive denominator — was treated as exaggeration in 2006 and reads as documentary now. The film is more useful as a thermometer than as a comedy.

**Key scenes**
- The opening eugenics-comedy montage.
- "Brawndo: It's got electrolytes."
- The Cabinet meeting.
- The final voiceover.

**Easy to miss** — The film's premise is uncomfortably eugenicist. Watch with that critique active — the satire is sharper if you separate the diagnosis (cultural decay) from the implied cause (the wrong people having children).

**The test** — Is the film's diagnosis right but its causal story wrong?

**Connections** — *Don't Look Up*, *Wall-E*-adjacent, *The Social Dilemma*.

## Love, Death & Robots (anthology, 2019– )

**What it's really about** — A kitchen-sink anthology that uses sci-fi vignettes to explore (in roughly equal measure) cosmic horror, military futurism, body anxiety, and absurdist comedy. The show's range is the pleasure; its hits ("Zima Blue," "The Witness," "Bad Travelling," "Good Hunting") justify the misses.

**Key scenes**
- "Zima Blue"'s pool-tile finale.
- "The Witness" loop.
- "Good Hunting" — the most thematically dense episode in the show.

**Easy to miss** — The show's better episodes are character pieces in genre clothing. The weaker ones are showcase animation with thin scripts. Watch episodically, by reputation, not in order.

**The test** — Which episode best uses sci-fi to say something only sci-fi can say?

**Connections** — *Black Mirror*, *Severance*, *2001*.

## Delete (Russian / Trigger-adjacent)

**What it's really about** — A young woman becomes addicted to a "delete" function for memories. The series is a chamber-piece on the inability of contemporary people to sit with discomfort, dressed in sci-fi premise.

**Key scenes**
- The first deletion.
- The reveal of cumulative loss.
- The therapist confrontation.

**Easy to miss** — The series treats its premise as a metaphor for contemporary numbing — alcohol, dating apps, doomscrolling. The "delete" is a literalization of dissociation.

**The test** — What gets lost that isn't visible?

**Connections** — *Eternal Sunshine*, *Eulogy*, *Common People*.

## The Lives of Others (2006)

**What it's really about** — A Stasi officer in 1984 East Berlin assigned to surveil a playwright slowly becomes the playwright's protector. The film argues that surveillance, fully entered into, is sometimes indistinguishable from love — and that the act of really listening to a life can transform the listener.

**Key scenes**
- The opening interrogation training.
- Wiesler hearing the Sonata for a Good Man.
- The typewriter under the floorboards.
- "Sonata für einen guten Menschen — for me?"

**Easy to miss** — The film is structurally a moral education in reverse. We start with Wiesler as a bureaucrat of state cruelty and end with him as a man who saved a life he was supposed to ruin. The "good man" of the title is him.

**The test** — When does Wiesler decide to protect Dreyman?

**Connections** — *The Pianist*, *Schindler's List*, *The Truman Show* (the watcher's moral status).
