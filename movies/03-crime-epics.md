# Crime Epics & Moral Decay

The crime film as moral thermometer. These are not really about cops and robbers; they're about how a society's stated values look when you photograph the people who exempt themselves from those values. Power, family, masculinity, and rot.

---

## The Godfather Trilogy (1972 / 1974 / 1990)

**What it's really about** — An American immigrant family's "legitimate business" arc, dramatized as the destruction of every soul who enters it. The trilogy is one long argument that there is no clean version of power; the cleaner Michael tries to make it, the more it costs him.

**Key scenes**
- I: The wedding-day baptism of violence (Sonny beating Carlo, the horse's head). The restaurant assassination. "I know it was you, Fredo."
- II: Vito's young arc on the streets of Little Italy intercut with Michael's domination of Vegas — origin and end-state, side-by-side. The Cuba "I saw a strange thing today" speech. The closing flashback to the family table, Michael alone.
- III: The opera finale — Michael learns of Mary's death and screams without sound. Whatever you think of III's pacing, that single image earns the trilogy's ending.

**Easy to miss** — Michael becomes Vito and Vito would have hated him. Vito's whole project was to give Michael the option of legitimacy ("Senator Corleone, Governor Corleone"). Michael's "success" is the failure of his father's plan.

**The test** — At what specific moment is Michael lost?

**Connections** — *Goodfellas*, *The Irishman*, *Once Upon a Time in America*, *Peaky Blinders*.

## Goodfellas (1990)

**What it's really about** — Crime as a working-class fast track. Henry isn't tempted by evil; he's tempted by free parking and good tables. The film's accomplishment is making you feel the appeal — and then the comedown.

**Key scenes**
- "As far back as I can remember, I always wanted to be a gangster."
- The Copa long take.
- The "funny how?" scene with Tommy.
- The cocaine-and-helicopter day.
- Henry, in witness protection, complaining he can't get good food anymore.

**Easy to miss** — Karen narrates too. Scorsese splits the storytelling between the gangster and the wife who married into it. Her arc — repulsion, fascination, complicity, exhaustion — is the moral mirror Henry doesn't have.

**The test** — Why does the film end with a complaint about egg noodles?

**Connections** — *Casino*, *The Wolf of Wall Street*, *The Departed*, *The Irishman*.

## The Irishman (2019)

**What it's really about** — Goodfellas, thirty years later, asking what crime looks like when nobody is watching anymore. Frank ends up in a nursing home, talking to a priest who doesn't know who Hoffa was. The film is about loyalty's terminal cost: an empty room and a door left ajar.

**Key scenes**
- Hoffa and Frank in pajamas eating ice cream — domestic warmth before the kill.
- The walk through the house in Detroit.
- Peggy's silence — she barely speaks all film.
- The final shot: door ajar, Frank waiting for someone who is never coming.

**Easy to miss** — Peggy is the moral judge of the film. Her refusal to talk to Frank after the Hoffa hit is the verdict the film respects. Scorsese does not give Frank absolution; he gives him a daughter who walked out.

**The test** — Why is the door left open at the end?

**Connections** — *Goodfellas*, *The Godfather II*, *Once Upon a Time in America*.

## The Departed (2006)

**What it's really about** — Identity as a job description. Both Costigan and Sullivan are paid to be other people for so long that the original self stops existing. The film is about the cost of becoming the role.

**Key scenes**
- The opening Costello monologue: "I don't want to be a product of my environment. I want my environment to be a product of me."
- The therapy scenes — the only place Costigan tells the truth.
- The elevator.
- The rat scuttling across the railing in the final shot.

**Easy to miss** — The film is built on Catholic guilt. Crosses, churches, fathers (literal and figurative) saturate the frame. Boston isn't a setting; it's a confessional.

**The test** — Which character actually wins?

**Connections** — *Goodfellas*, *Pulp Fiction*, *Memories of Murder*, *Infernal Affairs* (the original).

## Once Upon a Time in Hollywood (2019)

**What it's really about** — A love letter to a Hollywood that died with Tate, written in the form of a fairy tale that lets her live. The film is about the way pop culture gets to rewrite history because we want it to.

**Key scenes**
- Cliff at Spahn Ranch — pure suspense built from nothing but vibes.
- Sharon Tate watching herself in the theater.
- The flamethrower finale.
- Rick's tearful acting compliment from the little girl.

**Easy to miss** — The film's title (*Once Upon a Time*) is the move. Tarantino is openly telling you this is a fable. The catharsis at the end is the catharsis of fiction over fact, and the film knows it's a confession of helplessness, not heroism.

**The test** — Why does Tarantino spare Tate?

**Connections** — *Inglourious Basterds*, *Pulp Fiction*, *Django Unchained*.

## Pulp Fiction (1994)

**What it's really about** — Less plot than moral mathematics. The film cycles through characters at moments of grace (Jules, Butch) and damnation (Vincent). The non-linearity exists so you watch Vincent die, then watch him later choose his death by going back into the bathroom.

**Key scenes**
- The Ezekiel monologue.
- The diner robbery framing the film.
- The watch story.
- The dance at Jack Rabbit Slim's.
- The needle to Mia's heart.

**Easy to miss** — Jules is the protagonist. His arc is the only complete one — a moral conversion. Vincent's death is the cost of refusing what Jules accepts.

**The test** — What does Jules see that Vincent doesn't?

**Connections** — *The Departed*, *Inglourious Basterds*, *Once Upon a Time in Hollywood*.

---

## Inglourious Basterds (2009)

**What it's really about** — Cinema as a weapon. Tarantino literally kills Hitler with film prints in a movie theater. The film is about the moral fantasy of stories that are allowed to make history come out right.

**Key scenes**
- The opening farmhouse interrogation — possibly the best scene Tarantino ever wrote.
- The basement bar shootout.
- Shosanna's revenge in the projection booth.
- "I think this just might be my masterpiece."

**Easy to miss** — Landa is funnier than he's evil, which is the actual horror. Tarantino is showing you that charm is bureaucratized atrocity's preferred face.

**The test** — Why does Tarantino end with Aldo carving the swastika into Landa's forehead and breaking the fourth wall?

**Connections** — *Pulp Fiction*, *Django Unchained*, *Once Upon a Time in Hollywood*, *Schindler's List* (counter-text).

## Django Unchained (2012)

**What it's really about** — A revenge fantasy about American slavery, openly dressed in spaghetti western tropes. The film argues that genre cinema is one of the few cultural forms allowed to deliver righteous catharsis on subjects polite history won't.

**Key scenes**
- The Mandingo fight in Candie's parlor.
- The phrenology-skull dinner-table monologue.
- Stephen, the head house slave, recognizing Django before anyone else.
- The dynamited plantation finale.

**Easy to miss** — Stephen (Samuel L. Jackson) is the film's most ideologically interesting character — a man who has fully internalized the system and runs it more ruthlessly than its owners. He, not Candie, is the true antagonist.

**The test** — Why does Django blow up the house with no one inside?

**Connections** — *Inglourious Basterds*, *12 Years a Slave*, *The Hateful Eight*.

---

## The Wolf of Wall Street (2013)

**What it's really about** — A satire that the audience keeps cheering for. Scorsese shows you the appeal of Belfort's life so completely that the moral becomes whether *you* can put the popcorn down. The film is a test the viewer mostly fails.

**Key scenes**
- The Quaalude crawl.
- "Sell me this pen."
- The final shot — a seminar audience hungry for Belfort's secret.

**Easy to miss** — Naomi's exit is one of the few moments the film stops being funny. She is the only character who truly leaves him, and the film respects her for it without sentimentalizing.

**The test** — Why does Scorsese end on the seminar audience, not Belfort?

**Connections** — *Goodfellas*, *American Psycho*, *The Social Network*.

---

## Peaky Blinders (series)

**What it's really about** — Class, trauma, and the inherited violence of empire as it shows up in one Birmingham family. Tommy Shelby is a soldier who came home and never came home. Every "rise" he engineers makes him less a person.

**Key scenes**
- Tommy at the racetrack making his first power play.
- The "by order of the Peaky Blinders" tag, said and meant.
- Grace's death in the bar.
- The horse-by-the-river hallucination near the end.

**Easy to miss** — The horses, recurring. Tommy's only honest tenderness in the series is for animals; every human relationship is leveraged. The horses are who he was before the war.

**The test** — What does Tommy actually want?

**Connections** — *The Godfather*, *Breaking Bad*, *There Will Be Blood*.

---

## Breaking Bad (series)

**What it's really about** — Not "good man becomes bad." That's marketing. Walter is a furious, prideful man who finally gets the excuse he has been waiting for since Gray Matter. The cancer is the permission slip, not the cause.

**Key scenes**
- "I am the one who knocks."
- The fly episode.
- "Say my name."
- The finale: Walt admitting to Skyler, "I did it for me. I liked it. I was good at it."
- Hank in the desert.

**Easy to miss** — Skyler is right almost the whole show. Audiences hated her in real time because she was the moral mirror Walt couldn't break.

**The test** — When does Walt actually become Heisenberg — chronologically and morally?

**Connections** — *The Godfather*, *Goodfellas*, *Better Call Saul*, *No Country for Old Men*.

## One Battle After Another (2025, P.T. Anderson)

**What it's really about** — A burned-out former radical and his teenage daughter on the run from a fascist remnant of the movement he once belonged to. PTA's most explicitly political film: a generation of American leftists confronting the question of what their late-life respectability cost the next generation. Funny in places, devastating in others, structurally a chase film with a long memory.

**Key scenes**
- The opening commune sequence.
- The Christmas-Adam radio bit.
- The desert pursuit set-piece.
- The final reunion.

**Easy to miss** — The film is loosely an adaptation of Pynchon's *Vineland*. PTA has been working that book's territory (politics, fatherhood, paranoia, California) for years; this is the most direct version. Reading even a summary of *Vineland* before watching pays off.

**The test** — Whose battle is the title naming — the father's, the daughter's, or the country's?

**Connections** — *There Will Be Blood*, *Inherent Vice*, *The Dark Knight Rises*, *Taxi Driver*.

## There Will Be Blood (2007)

**What it's really about** — American capitalism as an act of total spiritual annihilation. Plainview doesn't compete with religion; he supplants it. Eli's brand of God and Plainview's brand of oil are the same product with different costumes.

**Key scenes**
- The first 15 wordless minutes.
- "I drink your milkshake!"
- The baptism scene where Plainview is forced to publicly humiliate himself.
- "I'm finished."

**Easy to miss** — H.W., the deaf adopted son, is the only honest relationship in the film, and Plainview destroys it not by accident but because he cannot tolerate love he didn't manufacture.

**The test** — Why does Plainview kill Eli at the end, specifically with a bowling pin?

**Connections** — *No Country for Old Men*, *The Godfather*, *Citizen Kane*-adjacent.
