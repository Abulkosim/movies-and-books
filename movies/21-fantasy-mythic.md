# Fantasy & Mythic Journeys

Why do we still need myths? These films take seriously that fantasy is not escapism — it's a way of telling moral truth at a scale realism can't reach. The hobbit, the wizard, the spirit-bath-house: they are all forms of the same ancient claim that the small actor can change the world.

---

## The Lord of the Rings (Trilogy, 2001–2003)

**What it's really about** — The corruption of power, told with unfashionable sincerity. Tolkien wrote against modernity (industrialization, mechanized war) and Jackson's films are honest to that. The Ring is not a metaphor for any one evil — it's the structure of the desire to fix the world by ruling it, and the films argue that desire is the actual evil.

**Key scenes**
- The Council of Elrond.
- "You shall not pass."
- Boromir's death.
- The lighting of the beacons.
- Sam carrying Frodo up Mount Doom.
- "I can't carry it for you, but I can carry you."

**Easy to miss** — Sam is the hero. The trilogy is told as if it were Frodo's, but every climactic moral act is Sam's. The films are honest that the working-class gardener who never wanted the adventure is the one who finishes it.

**The test** — Why does Frodo ultimately fail at the Crack of Doom?

**Connections** — *Harry Potter*, *Dune* (counter-text on the chosen-one), *The Hunger Games*.

## Harry Potter Series (2001–2011)

**What it's really about** — A boarding-school adventure that becomes, across eight films, a fascism-and-resistance narrative — pure-blood ideology, propaganda press, ministry capture, lists of "undesirables." The early films are children's fantasy; the later films are political thriller wearing Hogwarts paint.

**Key scenes**
- *Sorcerer's Stone*: the mirror of Erised.
- *Prisoner of Azkaban*: "Expecto Patronum" double-self.
- *Goblet of Fire*: Voldemort's return in the graveyard.
- *Half-Blood Prince*: Dumbledore on the tower.
- *Deathly Hallows Pt. 2*: "Always."

**Easy to miss** — Snape's arc is the franchise's hardest moral case. He is brave, cruel, devoted, vindictive — and the saga refuses to let any one of those cancel another. He is also the most adult character in a children's series.

**The test** — Why is the prophecy about Harry — and not Neville, who fit it equally well?

**Connections** — *The Lord of the Rings*, *The Hunger Games*, *Stranger Things*, *Bridge to Terabithia*.

## Spirited Away (2001, Miyazaki)

**What it's really about** — A young girl's parents are turned into pigs by their consumer greed; she has to work in a bathhouse for spirits to save them and herself. The film is a moral education about labor, name, and memory. Chihiro becomes Sen because the bathhouse takes her name; she has to remember her name to leave.

**Key scenes**
- The opening drive into the abandoned park.
- The parents at the empty restaurant.
- The first night with Haku.
- No-Face on the train.
- The pig-recognition test.

**Easy to miss** — The "stink spirit" is a polluted river spirit. Miyazaki is staging an environmentalism without lecture — the bathhouse cleans up what humans have done to nature, and the act of cleaning is presented as honorable work.

**The test** — Why does the bathhouse take Chihiro's name?

**Connections** — *Bridge to Terabithia*, *The Lord of the Rings*, *Inside Out*, *Grave of the Fireflies*.
