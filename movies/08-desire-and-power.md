# Desire & Power

Films built around the negotiation of desire — between people, between bodies, and between bodies and the social codes that try to discipline them. Some are honest about it; some pretend to be honest while reproducing the very fantasies they claim to interrogate.

---

## 365 Days (2020)

**What it's really about** — A glossy fantasy of forced courtship sold as romance. The film's surface is sex; its actual content is the abduction trope wearing high-fashion drag. Watching it is more interesting than the film itself: it's a case study in what mass-market erotic fantasy is willing to launder.

**Key scenes**
- The kidnapping prologue.
- The yacht scene most viewers came for.
- The "you can leave" speech that the plot then makes false.
- The cliffhanger ending.

**Easy to miss** — The film treats Stockholm-syndrome dynamics as romantic. Critics outside the film's fanbase have been unsparing for that reason; reading those critiques is part of getting what the film is actually doing in the culture.

**The test** — What is the film selling, beyond the plot?

**Connections** — *9½ Weeks*, *After Series*, *Babygirl* (a much smarter version of the same theme).

## After Series (2019–2023)

**What it's really about** — A young woman's repeated reconciliation with a "complicated" boy whose damage is repackaged as depth. The series is candid wish-fulfillment — Wattpad origins showing through — about the fantasy that loving someone hard enough fixes them.

**Key scenes**
- The bet reveal.
- The hotel-fight reconciliations (recurring).
- The wedding that doesn't happen.
- The various era-jumping epilogues.

**Easy to miss** — The series is structurally a romance novel: each book wants its own emotional climax, so the relationship has to break and reform on a metronome. That's a genre constraint, not a character truth.

**The test** — Why does the cycle never end?

**Connections** — *365 Days*, *Through My Window*, *5th Wave*-adjacent YA.

## Through My Window (2022 trilogy)

**What it's really about** — Class-coded teen fantasy: working-class girl, billionaire-family boy, parents in the way. The film is comfortable in its tropes and uses them efficiently. Don't read it for realism; read it for genre fluency.

**Key scenes**
- The first window meeting.
- The family confrontation at the dinner.
- The summer-villa interludes.

**Easy to miss** — The class barrier is there but never fully addressed. The films are not interested in the political content of the setup; they want the aesthetic of forbidden access.

**The test** — What does the film want you to want?

**Connections** — *After Series*, *365 Days*, *Anyone But You*.

## Sex/Life (2021–2023)

**What it's really about** — The cliché of the suburban-mom-bored-of-her-husband, played as a fantasy with awkwardly real consequences. Season 2 admits more than Season 1 wants to: that the affair is not a fix and that the husband isn't the problem so much as the whole life that came with him.

**Key scenes**
- The shower scene the show became famous for.
- The journal-as-confession.
- The "have you really read this?" scene.

**Easy to miss** — Cooper, the husband, is the most interesting character. Season 2 lets him become unfaithful too and refuses to make him the villain. The show is more honest about the symmetry of marital boredom than its early reputation suggests.

**The test** — Whose desire is the show actually centering?

**Connections** — *Babygirl*, *Big Little Lies*, *Marriage Story*.

## Addicted (2014)

**What it's really about** — A successful woman's sex addiction framed as both pathology and cri de coeur. The film tries to give the central character genuine interiority but keeps slipping into the tropes of erotic thriller, which makes it interesting as an index of how hard the genre fights against psychological seriousness.

**Key scenes**
- The early gallery encounter.
- The therapist sessions.
- The breakdown at the end.

**Easy to miss** — The film is partly about the way professional Black women's emotional needs are pathologized. It doesn't do the work fully, but the seed is there.

**The test** — Is Zoe sick or starved?

**Connections** — *Sex/Life*, *Babygirl*, *Shame*-adjacent.

## Babygirl (2024)

**What it's really about** — A CEO's affair with an intern, shot from her POV, asking what desire looks like for a woman of her power. Unlike the *365 Days* version of this fantasy, this film is interested in the discomfort — the husband knows, the marriage is examined, the "submission" is something she chose because her life made it impossible to ask for at home.

**Key scenes**
- The first office encounter and the milk scene.
- The hotel-room rules.
- The husband's confrontation.
- The therapy-session resolution.

**Easy to miss** — The film is partially a generational meditation on Gen X women raised in 90s feminism finding their own desire embarrassing to themselves. The shame is the actual subject.

**The test** — Why does the film insist on giving the husband a sympathetic arc?

**Connections** — *9½ Weeks*, *Sex/Life*, *Marriage Story*, *Good Luck to You Leo Grande*.

## Anora (2024)

**What it's really about** — Not a sex worker's Cinderella story. The film looks like that for its first act, then slowly reveals itself as a study of how the rich treat people as inventory. The wedding is a punchline; the real subject is the long second half where Ani gets dragged through the Russian American underworld she briefly thought she'd married into.

**Key scenes**
- The Vegas wedding montage.
- The home invasion.
- The Brighton Beach mother-in-law arrival.
- The final car scene with Igor.

**Easy to miss** — Igor is the film's moral center. He is the gangster's foot-soldier who treats Ani with more dignity than her husband ever did. The final scene is the film acknowledging that — and showing how unprepared Ani is to receive it.

**The test** — Why does Ani cry at the end?

**Connections** — *Past Lives*, *Capernaum*, *Babygirl*, *Pretty Woman* (counter-text).

## 9½ Weeks (1986)

**What it's really about** — The original mainstream prestige treatment of erotic submission, dated in some ways and surprisingly clear-eyed in others. The film argues that the game has an expiry date — the appeal of being subsumed wears off when you start to lose pieces of yourself you wanted to keep.

**Key scenes**
- The blindfolded fridge-feeding.
- The Joe Cocker striptease.
- The auction scene.
- The walk-out at the end.

**Easy to miss** — Elizabeth leaves. The film is often remembered as a fantasy; it's actually a leaving story. The whole erotic apparatus exists to give weight to the no.

**The test** — Why does Elizabeth leave?

**Connections** — *Babygirl*, *Don't Move*, *Blue Valentine*.

---

## Good Luck to You, Leo Grande (2022)

**What it's really about** — A retired widow hires a young sex worker to give her experiences her marriage didn't. The film is a chamber piece — almost entirely two people in a hotel room — about pleasure as a civic right that older women have been quietly denied.

**Key scenes**
- The first awkward arrival.
- The list of "things she wants to do."
- The mirror scene at the end.

**Easy to miss** — The film is aggressively non-judgmental about sex work. Leo is a professional with boundaries; the film respects them, and respects Nancy for respecting them too.

**The test** — Whose pleasure is the film centering — and is it allowed to be both?

**Connections** — *Anora*, *Babygirl*, *The Intouchables*.
