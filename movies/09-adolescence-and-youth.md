# Adolescence & Youth

Why does growing up feel like a wound? These films treat adolescence as the period in which the world first refuses to be what you expected, and they take seriously the idea that you don't really survive it whole — you survive it changed.

---

## The Perks of Being a Wallflower (2012)

**What it's really about** — Charlie's depression and trauma are not "moods" — they are a hidden architecture beneath the friend-group story. The film's late reveal isn't a twist; it's the keystone the whole picture has been built on. The "we are infinite" line is earned by what comes after, not before.

**Key scenes**
- The tunnel ride and *Heroes*.
- The Rocky Horror cast.
- The hospitalization sequence.
- "We accept the love we think we deserve."

**Easy to miss** — The film is told entirely through Charlie's letters to a "friend." We never know who that friend is. The conceit suggests Charlie is, all along, narrating to a self he is trying to assemble.

**The test** — Why does Charlie write to a stranger?

**Connections** — *All the Bright Places*, *13 Reasons Why*, *Aftersun*, *Adolescence*.

## All the Bright Places (2020)

**What it's really about** — The asymmetry of recovery. Violet is grieving; Finch is bipolar. The film treats the relationship as both a real love and a real hazard, and is honest that "saving each other" is a fantasy that costs the saver a person.

**Key scenes**
- The bridge meeting.
- The wandering road-trip stops.
- The closet.
- The beach at the end.

**Easy to miss** — The film is faithful to the book in not letting Violet "save" Finch. The point is that mental illness is not romantic, even when love is real. This is a hard line to walk; some viewers feel it lands as romanticization anyway. Worth examining your own reaction.

**The test** — Why does Finch leave Violet the wandering map?

**Connections** — *13 Reasons Why*, *The Perks of Being a Wallflower*, *Five Feet Apart*.

## Spontaneous (2020)

**What it's really about** — Senior year as actual existential horror. Classmates start spontaneously combusting; the film uses the conceit as a metaphor for how adolescence already feels — random loss, the unfairness of who survives, the impossibility of planning. It is one of the most accidentally pandemic-era films of its decade.

**Key scenes**
- The first explosion in the classroom.
- The CDC quarantine sequence.
- The road-trip post-burst.
- The graduation monologue.

**Easy to miss** — The film is structured as a comedy. The horror works precisely because the tone is teen-movie-bright. The grief slips in under the genre.

**The test** — Why is the film a comedy?

**Connections** — *13 Reasons Why*, *The Perks of Being a Wallflower*, *Adolescence*.

## Bridge to Terabithia (2007)

**What it's really about** — Imagination as a survival mechanism for kids whose home lives are too small. When tragedy comes, the film is uncompromising: the kingdom doesn't bring her back. What it does is leave behind a habit of seeing that the surviving child can pass on.

**Key scenes**
- The first crossing into Terabithia.
- The classroom friendship.
- The death (offscreen, brutal in its restraint).
- The little sister entering Terabithia at the end.

**Easy to miss** — The film is more about Jess's father than viewers usually remember. The closing reconciliation isn't sentimental; it's the start of the dad becoming able to be a dad. Leslie's death cracked him too.

**The test** — Why does Jess invite his sister into Terabithia at the end?

**Connections** — *Inside Out*, *Aftersun*, *The Perks of Being a Wallflower*.

## Euphoria (series, 2019– )

**What it's really about** — Addiction, gender, and self-image among kids growing up under a camera. The show's stylization (the makeup, the lighting, the music) is part of its argument: this generation experiences its own life cinematically, and the show photographs that experience.

**Key scenes**
- Rue's first relapse.
- The carnival episode.
- Jules's POV special episode.
- Rue's holiday-special two-hander with Ali in the diner.

**Easy to miss** — The diner episodes (Rue with Ali, Jules with the therapist) are the show's spine. They strip the visual style away on purpose to argue that under all the maximalism, the show is moral, conversational, and worried about its characters.

**The test** — What is Rue actually addicted to?

**Connections** — *13 Reasons Why*, *Adolescence*, *Requiem for a Dream*, *Trigger*.

## 13 Reasons Why (series, 2017–2020)

**What it's really about** — Surface: a teen suicide explained by tapes. Underneath: a culture's refusal to take adolescent pain seriously until it's too late, plus a structural critique of the school as a closed accountability system. The show has been criticized — fairly — for romanticizing the death; it is worth watching with that critique active.

**Key scenes**
- The opening-locker tape distribution.
- The bathroom scene later seasons retcon.
- The trial sequences in S2.
- Hannah's final episode (controversial; later edited).

**Easy to miss** — The show's pivot in S2 is partly an admission that the original framing was dangerous. The later seasons are a mass-media object actively trying to repair its own initial mistake.

**The test** — Whose responsibility was Hannah's death?

**Connections** — *Adolescence*, *The Perks of Being a Wallflower*, *Euphoria*.

## Adolescence (Netflix, 2025)

**What it's really about** — A 13-year-old boy stabs a girl. Each of four episodes is a single take. The show does not ask "how was he raised badly?" — it asks "what is the new internet doing to boys in particular, and what are the adults around them refusing to see?" It's a procedural about the manosphere that no procedural was equipped to write before.

**Key scenes**
- E1: the home arrest.
- E2: the school visit.
- E3: the psychologist interview, almost the entire episode is two faces.
- E4: the family aftermath.

**Easy to miss** — The single-take form is doing thematic work. The show is denying you cuts because it is denying the adults in the story (and you, the viewer) the comfort of looking away.

**The test** — What did the parents not see?

**Connections** — *13 Reasons Why*, *Baby Reindeer*, *Joker*, *The Social Dilemma*.

## Aftersun (2022)

**What it's really about** — A grown woman trying, twenty years later, to assemble what she didn't notice as a child: her young father's depression on the holiday before he died. The film is about the limits of memory as a tool for love.

**Key scenes**
- The karaoke "Losing My Religion" Calum refuses to sing.
- The father teaching her self-defense.
- The video-camera footage interludes.
- The strobe-lit dance floor where adult Sophie tries to reach him.

**Easy to miss** — The film never confirms Calum's suicide, but the grammar makes it certain. The opening flash of Sophie watching the home-video years later, alone in a flat with her own daughter, is everything.

**The test** — What is the strobing dance floor showing us?

**Connections** — *Past Lives*, *Hamnet*, *Wild Strawberries*, *Detachment*.

---

## Normal People (series, 2020)

**What it's really about** — Class, intimacy, and the private dialect two people develop in adolescence and then can't quite escape. The show is unusual in that the romance is also the trauma: each protagonist's deepest comfort and deepest harm are the same person.

**Key scenes**
- The school-party first conversation.
- The Trinity dinner where the class dynamic flips.
- The neck-chain.
- The final phone call.

**Easy to miss** — The sex scenes are doing structural work — they are the only place either character communicates without coding. Cutting them, as some viewers want, would gut the show's argument about what intimacy gives them that nothing else does.

**The test** — Why don't they end up together?

**Connections** — *Blue Valentine*, *Aftersun*, *Past Lives*, *6 Years*.

## Stranger Things (Seasons 1–5)

**What it's really about** — Eighties nostalgia as a vehicle for the actual subject: a small town's children growing up in the shadow of a federal program that won't admit it exists. Underneath the genre callbacks is a story about adolescence under government secrecy and parental absence — the kids are on their own because the adults are either complicit or oblivious.

**Key scenes**
- S1: Eleven in the bathtub finding Will.
- S2: Bob's death.
- S3: the mall fire and Hopper's letter.
- S4: Max and "Running Up That Hill."
- S5: Eddie / final reckoning with Vecna (depending on what beats land).

**Easy to miss** — The show is partly about Reagan-era cultural amnesia. The Soviet plot, the MK-ULTRA-style program, the "satanic panic" — these are not random. The show is set inside the very 80s the 80s wouldn't talk about.

**The test** — Why is the show set in the 80s, specifically?

**Connections** — *Dark*, *Adolescence*, *The Perks of Being a Wallflower*.
