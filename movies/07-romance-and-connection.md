# Romance & Connection

These films are less about heartbreak than about the strange thing that happens when two people are actually paying attention to each other. They take connection seriously as a subject in itself — what it is, why it's rare, why it sometimes lasts and sometimes can't.

---

## The Before Trilogy — Before Sunrise (1995) / Before Sunset (2004) / Before Midnight (2013)

**What it's really about** — Three films, nine years apart, on the same two people. Together they argue that a relationship is not an event but a long conversation that you can either keep having or stop having. The trilogy is the most honest study of partnership in modern cinema because it makes you wait nine years for the next chapter the way you wait nine years inside a marriage.

**Key scenes**
- *Sunrise*: the listening-booth in the record store; the pretend phone calls.
- *Sunset*: the waltz / "Just in Time" record at the end. "Baby… you are gonna miss that plane."
- *Midnight*: the hotel-room fight — the trilogy's bravest scene, dismantling the romance the first two films built.

**Easy to miss** — Linklater shoots in long takes specifically to make the chemistry indistinguishable from the writing. The films feel improvised because the actors co-wrote them with him; what feels like spontaneity is decades of craft.

**The test** — Are Jesse and Celine still in love at the end of *Midnight*?

**Connections** — *Past Lives*, *La La Land*, *Lost in Translation*, *Marriage Story*.

## Notting Hill (1999)

**What it's really about** — The class fantasy beneath rom-coms: an ordinary man's life made important by a famous woman noticing him. The film is unusually honest that fame is a kind of class, and that the obstacle to the romance is not "personality" but the structural impossibility of two such different lives.

**Key scenes**
- The seasons walk through Portobello Road.
- The press-conference finale: "I'm just a girl, standing in front of a boy…"
- The bench at the end.

**Easy to miss** — The supporting cast (especially Spike, the mad housemate) is the film's secret weapon. They're not comic relief — they're the texture of the unfamous life Anna is choosing into.

**The test** — Why does the film end at the bench, not the press conference?

**Connections** — *The Idea of You*, *Anyone But You*, *Pride and Prejudice*.

## Pride and Prejudice (2005, Wright)

**What it's really about** — Less the love story than the economic structure that requires the love story. Five sisters with no inheritance must marry; the film treats this as the survival drama it actually is, with romance as the lucky escape valve for two of them.

**Key scenes**
- The first dance and Darcy's "tolerable, I suppose."
- The first proposal in the rain.
- The hand-flex after he helps her into the carriage.
- The dawn meeting on the moor.

**Easy to miss** — The film is saturated with hands. Touch is rationed by social code, so every brush of skin is loaded. Wright knows it; the camera knows it; you should know it.

**The test** — Is the romance possible without the inheritance?

**Connections** — *Notting Hill*, *Anna Karenina*, *Sense and Sensibility*.

## Anyone But You (2023)

**What it's really about** — Standard enemies-to-lovers structure, but the film is interested in something subtler: how people who are attracted to each other build elaborate cover stories to avoid admitting it. The "pretending to date" plot is just the explicit version of what the characters do internally.

**Key scenes**
- The bathroom-door-locked first meet.
- The Australia destination wedding.
- The cliff jump.
- The closing reconciliation under "Unwritten."

**Easy to miss** — The film is structurally *Much Ado About Nothing* — the rom-com's classical bones showing through. The screenplay quietly leans on that.

**The test** — Why doesn't the "fake dating" deception end the film like it does in most rom-coms?

**Connections** — *Notting Hill*, *No Strings Attached*, *The Idea of You*.

## The Idea of You (2024)

**What it's really about** — A 40-year-old woman risking the social cost of dating a 24-year-old pop star. The film treats age-gap discomfort directly — not the romance, but the audience and family reaction to the romance — and that's the honest subject.

**Key scenes**
- The first private dinner.
- The paparazzi outing.
- The daughter's reaction.
- The years-later reunion at the gallery.

**Easy to miss** — The film is sympathetic to the daughter's discomfort. Solène's choice to step back isn't martyrdom — the film genuinely thinks the costs to her child are real, and the reunion only happens after the daughter has grown up.

**The test** — What does Solène actually have to give up?

**Connections** — *Notting Hill*, *Anyone But You*, *Lost in Translation*, *Babygirl* (counter-text).

## The Intern (2015)

**What it's really about** — Surface: a 70-year-old intern at a startup. Underneath: a film about loneliness in working women's lives that no one wants to call by its name. Ben isn't a comic device; he is the listener Jules's marriage and culture deny her.

**Key scenes**
- The handkerchief lesson.
- The break-in to delete the email.
- The hotel-room conversation about the affair.
- The morning tai chi.

**Easy to miss** — The husband's affair is the film's real plot. The intern story is a frame for Jules confronting a marriage she has been outearning for years. The film is more grown-up than its trailer.

**The test** — Why is Ben the only person Jules can talk to?

**Connections** — *As Good as It Gets*, *About Time*, *The Intouchables*.

## No Strings Attached (2011)

**What it's really about** — The negotiation generation's attempt to have intimacy without the costs of intimacy. The film's thesis is that the deal can't actually hold — feelings keep showing up uninvited — and it lands with surprising honesty for a Hollywood comedy.

**Key scenes**
- The "rules" written on a napkin.
- The "period mix" CD.
- The breakup on the porch.
- The "we should be a couple" final scene.

**Easy to miss** — The film is structurally about Emma's commitment phobia, not Adam's pursuit. He is the catalyst; she has the arc. Most viewers remember it as his film. It isn't.

**The test** — Why does Emma agree to the rules in the first place?

**Connections** — *Friends with Benefits*, *Anyone But You*, *500 Days of Summer*.

---

## Lost in Translation (2003)

**What it's really about** — Two people, jet-lagged in Tokyo, recognizing each other as the only other person in the room who is awake. The film is about the kind of intimacy that can only exist in transit — and what it teaches you about the sleepwalking life waiting back home.

**Key scenes**
- The karaoke night.
- The whisky-bar ramen.
- The "more intense than that" filming-the-commercial bit.
- The whispered goodbye in the street.

**Easy to miss** — Coppola does not tell you what Bob whispers, on purpose. The unspoken is the medium of the film. To "decode" it (and people online have tried) is to misunderstand the whole point.

**The test** — Why doesn't the film let us hear what he says?

**Connections** — *Past Lives*, *Before Sunrise*, *Her*, *Aftersun*.

## Her (2013)

**What it's really about** — Not whether you can love a computer. Whether you can be a good partner to a person who cannot fit inside your interior life. The OS is a technologized version of an intimacy mismatch most relationships eventually face.

**Key scenes**
- Theodore writing letters for strangers — the film's quiet thesis on outsourced intimacy.
- The double-date dinner.
- The "I am yours and I am not yours" speech.
- The final rooftop with Amy.

**Easy to miss** — Samantha's polyamory revelation isn't sci-fi weirdness. It's a metaphor for the way many partners do, in fact, exceed their relationships — and how the diminished party is left unable to contest it.

**The test** — Why does the film end with Theodore and Amy on the roof, not with Samantha?

**Connections** — *Lost in Translation*, *Marriage Story*, *Eternal Sunshine*, *Blade Runner 2049*.

---

## Love Actually (2003)

**What it's really about** — Christmas as the deadline that makes British people finally say what they meant. The film weaves nine stories of love at different stages — most of them failing, some of them succeeding, one or two creepy by modern standards — to argue that the whole human range counts as the same thing.

**Key scenes**
- The cards-on-the-doorstep declaration.
- The "to me, you are perfect."
- Sarah choosing her brother over Karl.
- Emma Thompson's bedroom cry to Joni Mitchell — the film's secret center of gravity.

**Easy to miss** — The film has a darker edge than its reputation suggests. Several of the stories don't have happy endings; the Emma Thompson / Alan Rickman thread is, on rewatch, the most adult of them all.

**The test** — Which of the nine stories is the one the film actually believes in?

**Connections** — *About Time*, *The Holiday*, *Notting Hill*.
